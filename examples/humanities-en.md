# Humanities Test Case: Craft of Research Skill Full-Process Test

> **Scene:** Cultural Studies / Memory Studies
> **Researcher Profile:** A master's student who wants to study "how second-generation Chinese immigrants construct cultural identity on social media." Has read some literature but feels "everyone is working on this topic, I can't say what makes mine different."
> **Test Goal:** Run through Dimension 1 → 2 → 3 → 4/5 → 6 → Self-Check, verifying the skill's guidance effectiveness in humanities & social science scenarios.
> **Estimated Dialogue Duration:** 14-16 rounds
> **Difficulty:** ⭐⭐⭐

---

## Round 1

> 💡 **Hard Gate: The researcher expresses anxiety that "this topic has been done to death." The guide lets her describe her state before offering direction.**

**Guide:**
Tell me about your current research state. No need to be formal. Just say what you're thinking right now.

**Researcher:**
I want to study how second-generation Chinese immigrants construct cultural identity on social media, for example on Xiaohongshu (RED) and Instagram. But the problem is, this topic feels like it's been done by so many people already. "Third space," "hybridity," and "cross-cultural identity" all feel like the same vocabulary. I don't know what new I can say.

## Round 2

> 💡 **The researcher has a direction but feels "there's no innovation" → This is where the Creative Framework applies. The guide uses the Three-Part Frame to help the researcher turn a vague direction into concrete coordinates, rather than giving direct answers.**

**Guide:**
"Being done before" doesn't necessarily mean there's no place for you. Sometimes it's a sign that this is a conversation worth continuing.

Let's do a positioning exercise. Use this Three-Part Frame to articulate what you can currently say clearly:

> I study **______** because I want to understand **______**, so that people can better understand **______**.

You might not be able to fill in the third sentence. That's fine. Fill in the first two first.

**Researcher:**
I study cultural identity construction of second-generation Chinese immigrants on social media, because I want to understand how the cultural symbols and discursive strategies they use on Xiaohongshu and Instagram differ from those of their parents' generation.

**Guide:**
And the third sentence: after understanding this difference, what can it help people better understand?

**Researcher:**
Hmm... it could help people better understand the role of "intergenerational differentiation" in immigrant identity? Or, more precisely, we've been talking about "transnational identity," but the "transnational" experience for the second generation and the first generation may not be the same thing. The first generation chooses between Chinese and Western cultures; the second generation may not even feel the need to "choose."

**Guide:**
That third sentence has something.

You just said "I feel there's nothing new to say," but now you've articulated a potential new angle yourself: the difference between the first and second generations in identity construction is not quantitative but qualitative. The first generation's "cross-cultural" experience involves switching between two systems; the second generation's "cross-cultural" experience may be about growing directly within a third space that has already been fused.

This idea itself is a potential entry point. We'll come back to refine it later. First, let's do a core test.

## Round 3

> 💡 **The researcher has identified her potential angle → The "I Don't Care" Check stress-tests it while the Creative Framework scan begins.**

**Guide:**
Let's start with an "I Don't Care" Check.

If you presented your research question at an academic conference: "How does the cultural identity construction of second-generation Chinese immigrants on Xiaohongshu and Instagram differ from that of the first generation?" Would the audience respond with "I don't care"? Why?

**Researcher:**
People working on immigration studies would care, because this is a new question. Most of the literature still focuses on the first generation. People working on social media studies would also care, because platform differences (Xiaohongshu vs. Instagram) may influence how identity is expressed.

But some might say you're just adding another case study. You say "different," but "different" doesn't mean much by itself. You need to clarify why this difference matters.

**Guide:**
This self-questioning is good. You've already answered half of it yourself. Your study needs to answer not "what's different," but "what this difference reveals."

Now, let's use the Creative Framework to scan your position in this academic conversation. In the literature you've read, what do you agree with? What do you disagree with? Is there anything you feel is "both are right but nobody has connected them"?

**Researcher:**
I agree with Hall's view that identity is a "process" not an "essence." This framework applies to both generations. What I disagree with is that much of the literature directly applies the analytical framework for first-generation identity to the second generation, as if "diaspora" and "hybridity" mean the same thing for everyone. But in reality, the media environment has changed (the first generation mainly watched TV or read newspapers, the second generation uses social media), so the specific mechanisms of the "process" are completely different.

I think what nobody has done is introducing "media platform differences" as a variable into the comparative analysis of intergenerational identity construction. Existing literature either studies intergenerational identity (but doesn't look at media) or studies social media identity (but doesn't do generational comparison).

## Round 4

> 💡 **The researcher arrives at a "creative synthesis" position. It is richer than merely applying "agree/disagree." The guide introduces the So What? Cascade.**

**Guide:**
This is a good position. You're not simply agreeing or disagreeing with anyone. You're building a bridge between two existing conversations: intergenerational identity studies and social media studies. That in itself is an academic contribution.

Now let's run through the So What? Cascade. Layer by layer, we'll work through your chain of significance.

**Layer 1:** "I found that the identity construction strategies of second-generation immigrants on social media differ from those of the first generation. Not a simple intergenerational difference, but a qualitative difference driven by media platform variation."

→ **So what?**

Why should anyone care about this finding?

**Researcher:**
Because if media platforms themselves are shaping the way identity is expressed, then we can no longer say "identity is transmedia." Instead, we should say "identity is mediated under specific platform conditions."

**Guide:**
Layer 2. You've actually challenged the default assumption of "transmedia identity."

→ **You challenged this assumption. So what?**

**Researcher:**
That means identity studies can no longer only analyze content (what they posted), but must also analyze the technological affordances of platforms (what forms of expression the platform enables or constrains). For example, Xiaohongshu's image-text mode vs. Instagram's visual-driven mode. They impose completely different requirements on identity narratives.

**Researcher:**
So existing cross-cultural adaptation theories (such as Berry's acculturation model) may need updating. **Guide:**
Layer 4.

This... I've thought about this but haven't fully sorted it out. You've now hit the methodological level, and that's a strong point. The fifth and final layer. Looking further out, who would find this insight practically useful?

**Researcher:**
It's also meaningful for people working on immigration policy and social services. If you want to use social media to understand immigrant community needs, you first need to know that the platform itself is filtering what information is available. ## Round 6

> 💡 **The significance chain is complete → The guide introduces the C-P-R framework to help the researcher organize her ideas into a communicable research story. This is also a transition from "thinking" to "saying."**

**Guide:**
Good. You now have a well-rounded significance structure. Let's tighten it into a 30-second research story using the C-P-R Framework:

- **Context (Common Ground):** "About immigrant identity, we already know that..."
- **Problem (Gap + Cost):** "But the current limitation is..., and if left unaddressed..."
- **Response (Your Contribution):** "So I study..."

Give it a try.

**Researcher:**
Context. There is already extensive research on cultural identity construction of second-generation immigrants, but most of it applies the analytical framework of the first generation. Problem. However, these studies assume that "identity construction" is a unified process unaffected by media, ignoring the fact that social media platforms' technological affordances are fundamentally changing how young immigrants express their identity. Without filling this gap, our understanding of the second generation may remain trapped in the concepts of the previous generation. Response. So I compare the cultural symbol use and discursive strategies of the first and second generations on Xiaohongshu and Instagram, to analyze how platform technological conditions mediate identity construction across different generations.

**Guide:**
That Response sounds like it could be the main title of a thesis.

## Round 7

> 💡 **The researcher states the research story clearly → The argument building phase begins, starting with the core claim.**

**Guide:**
Good, now we enter the argumentation phase. Use the "Although/Even though" pattern to write your core claim. This simultaneously clarifies what you are challenging and where you stand.

> Although ______, ______, because ______.

Give it a try.

**Researcher:**
Although identity studies of second-generation immigrants have widely applied theoretical frameworks such as "third space" or "hybridity," most studies have overlooked the mediating role of digital platforms' technological affordances in intergenerational identity expression, because the media environment itself (image-text vs. visual, algorithmic recommendation vs. active search) fundamentally sets the possible range of identity narratives.

**Guide:**
This sounds like the first paragraph of a literature review. But it does capture your core theoretical claim. Let's unpack it and see what evidence each part needs.

The three things you said:
1. "Existing research has overlooked the role of platforms." You need literature evidence to demonstrate this "gap."
2. "Platform technological affordances influence how identity is expressed." You need empirical evidence (content analysis plus platform feature analysis).
3. "The first and second generations have different identity construction mechanisms because of different platform environments." You need comparative evidence.

These are the three pillars of your thesis. What do you have so far?

**Researcher:**
I have literature analysis (doing a systematic review) and preliminary content classification coding (I've collected 50 second-generation immigrant account posts each from Xiaohongshu and Instagram). But I haven't done platform affordance analysis yet. For example, how Xiaohongshu's dual-column image-text layout vs. Instagram's single-column visual feed affects narrative form.

**Guide:**
This gap itself can become one of your analytical dimensions.

## Round 8

> 💡 **The researcher reveals an evidence gap → The guide does not use "you're missing evidence" to worry her, but helps her turn this gap into an element of research design. It also introduces Evidence assessment.**

**Guide:**
So you are actually building a mixed-methods argument:

- **Qualitative methods:** Thematic coding of post content (analyzing "what is being said")
- **Platform comparison:** Analyzing the technological affordance differences of the two platforms (analyzing "under what conditions it is being said")
- **Intergenerational comparison:** Contrasting your findings with existing first-generation immigrant research (analyzing "who it is different from")

What you most need to do right now is step two, platform affordance analysis. What are the common approaches for this analysis?

**Researcher:**
I could use a technological affordance framework (such as affordance theory's dimensions of visibility, persistence, association, etc.) to systematically compare the two platforms. Or a more direct approach: comparing content differences of the same user on Xiaohongshu and Instagram (if they have dual-platform accounts).

**Guide:**
The second approach is very powerful. The same person displaying different identities on different platforms. The difference itself is the finding.

Now let's do an **Ethos Check**: if someone who disagrees with your core hypothesis reads your paper, would they feel you've treated dissenting views fairly?

Your core hypothesis is "platforms shape identity expression." How might it be challenged?

**Researcher:**
Some might say you're applying a technological determinist framework, underestimating user agency. People use the same platform for completely different things. Some use Xiaohongshu to look up recipes, others use it for political mobilization. You can't say "Xiaohongshu leads to X." You have to say "certain features of Xiaohongshu, combined with certain usage patterns, produce Y."

**Guide:**
This response is good. You've anticipated a strong objection. How would you incorporate this qualification into your argument?

**Researcher:**
I would say I'm not arguing that platforms determine identity, but that platforms set the conditions of possibility. On the same Xiaohongshu, some people display a "polished international life," others display "my struggle between two worlds." The difference shows that the platform hasn't determined the content, but both types of content are "equally" presented in the same image-text format on Xiaohongshu.

## Round 9

**Guide:**
Good. You've developed a protective awareness of your argument. Now let's look at it using the Significance Level Scale. Where does your contribution fall?

| Level | Essence |
|------|--------|
| 1. New Information | Provides new facts |
| 2. Resolves Confusion | Resolves contradictions or uncertainties in the field |
| 3. Shakes Foundations | Challenges long-unquestioned basic assumptions |

Where do you think your research falls?

**Researcher:**
I'd say between 2 and 3. Level 2 (resolves confusion). I'm answering "how is identity construction on social media different from that in traditional media." Level 3 (shakes foundations). But going further, I may be challenging the basic assumption that "identity is a unified concept." If platforms can "orchestrate" identity expression, then the concept of "authentic identity" itself may need to be re-theorized. But that part may be beyond the scope of this single paper.

**Guide:**
The fact that you can say "beyond the scope of this paper" yourself shows you have good judgment. You're in a good position now: using Level 2 evidence to fight a Level 2 battle, while pointing to Level 3 possibilities without overreaching.

## Round 10

> 💡 **The researcher's reasoning has improved → Introduce Dimension 4 (Ethics) and Dimension 5 (Evolution). Research ethics in the humanities and social sciences are often more complex than in the sciences, worth spending an extra round on.**

**Guide:**
Good. Let's quickly go through two dimensions.

**Dimension 4 (Ethics):** What ethical considerations does your research have?

**Researcher:**
Several. First, although posts on Xiaohongshu and Instagram are public content, users may not expect their posts to be used for research. I need to consider the boundaries of using public data for analysis, especially when involving minors and vulnerable groups. Second, immigrant communities themselves may be a sensitive group. I cannot reinforce a stereotypical framework like "the first generation is traditional, the second generation is modern." Third, my research subjects display an "orchestrated self" on platforms. When using this for analysis, I need to acknowledge this within an ethical framework, not pretending I've captured the "real them."

**Guide:**
These reflections are solid. Dimension 5 (Evolution). If your research were accepted, what would be the most interesting new question to pursue next?

**Researcher:**
A few directions. First, the role of algorithmic recommendation systems in identity construction. What you see now is what users post, but you don't know why they post it. Did they get more engagement with certain content, so they keep posting similar content? Second, the relationship between platform commercialization and identity expression. Xiaohongshu itself is a "lifestyle platform" that encourages a consumerist identity narrative. How much of the second-generation immigrants' "cultural identity" is being guided by commercial logic? Third, can this framework be extended to other immigrant groups? I'm currently focused on second-generation Chinese immigrants, but the relationship between intergenerational identity and platform mechanisms in other groups may be similar.

**Guide:**
All three directions are interesting. The third, extending the framework to other groups, could become your next article, or even part of your PhD research plan.

## Round 13

> 💡 **Dimension 6 (Delivery): Writing planning. The researcher has completed the platform affordance analysis and has a full evidence chain, but faces a new challenge: how to structure the paper. Humanities and social science arguments don't follow the standard IMRaD format like in the sciences, and the researcher's "double comparison" (generation × platform) poses organizational challenges. The guide focuses on organizational structure (§11) and narrative arc (§12).**

**Guide:**
Good. Have you completed the platform affordance analysis now? How does it feel?

**Researcher:**
I've done it. The results are quite interesting. I found that the identity construction of the same user on the two platforms does show systematic differences: on Instagram, they emphasize "visualized individual identity" (personal photos, style outfits); on Xiaohongshu, they emphasize "functional community identity" (guides, experience sharing). But on both platforms, they avoid directly expressing labeled identities like "I am Chinese" or "I am a foreigner." My initial conclusion is that the platform does not dictate what identity they display, but influences *how* they display it.

**Guide:**
This finding itself is very valuable, especially the distinction between "how to display" and "what to display."

Now the question is how do you organize this paper? You have three sets of comparative variables: generation (first vs. second), platform (Xiaohongshu vs. Instagram), and analytical dimension (content themes vs. affordances). Readers can easily get lost.

How are you planning to structure it?

**Researcher:**
I've considered two options.

Option A. By platform: first present findings from Xiaohongshu (first-generation vs. second-generation comparison), then findings from Instagram. Option B. By analytical dimension: first present content coding results (what identities the two generations displayed on the two platforms), then platform affordance analysis results (how the two platforms influenced modes of display).

**Guide:**
The problem with Option A is that readers may have forgotten the Instagram section by the time they finish the Xiaohongshu section, and the comparative dimension is weakened. The problem with Option B is that your core claim is about the "platform × generation interaction," but separating by dimension effectively splits the interaction across two chapters.

I suggest you consider a "claim-driven structure." Each step serves to advance your core argument:

1. **Starting point: What is the intergenerational difference?** → Present only the generational dimension differences in content coding (set platforms aside for now)
2. **Refinement: What role do platforms play?** → Introduce platform affordance analysis, showing differences across platforms within the same generation
3. **Synthesis: The generation × platform interaction** → Bring both threads together, presenting your core finding

This structure lets the reader know at each step "why we are looking at this data."

> 💡 **The guide recommends a "progressive" structure. From simple to complex, each step builds on the previous one. This better matches the reader's cognitive path than organizing by topic or by method.**

**Researcher:**
This structure is interesting. But my concern is that this would make it seem like I'm "giving the conclusion before presenting the evidence." The traditional paper order is "methods → results → discussion."

**Guide:**
In social science papers, a "claim-driven structure" does not mean giving the conclusion first. In the first section (starting point), you present only coded data, without interpretation. In the second section (refinement), you introduce a second analytical dimension. Only in the third section (synthesis) do you bring the two threads together. At each step, the reader only sees new analysis and doesn't feel you're "pre-judging the answer."

Moreover, your narrative arc becomes clear: **Starting point (puzzle: intergenerational differences exist but the cause is unclear) → Conflict (the media environments of the first and second generations are completely different) → Development (analysis reveals that platform affordances change the mode of identity construction) → Resolution (intergenerational difference is essentially platform-mediated difference)**. This arc itself is a good story.

## Round 14

> 💡 **Structure is settled → Discuss specific writing issues: paragraph writing (§13) and style (§15). In humanities and social science papers, weaving theory and data together is the most common writing difficulty. The guide checks whether the researcher can naturally integrate abstract concepts with concrete evidence.**

**Guide:**
Good, the structure is clear. Let's talk about the specifics of writing.

Your paper requires extensive interweaving of theory and evidence. For example, on a single page you might simultaneously mention Hall's identity-as-process theory, Xiaohongshu's functional affordances, and post content you've captured from a specific account. This places high demands on your writing. Readers can easily feel "where am I" when jumping between abstraction and concreteness.

Have you thought about how to make these transitions naturally within a paragraph?

**Researcher:**
Hmm... I usually start with a theoretical concept, then say "for example...", then add analysis. But it's true that I feel when writing that the theory paragraphs and data analysis paragraphs seem like they were written by two different people.

**Guide:**
This is one of the most common difficulties in humanities and social science writing. An effective technique is the **anchor sentence** (§13): the first sentence of each paragraph simultaneously clarifies "which theoretical concept are we discussing" and "which dataset are we looking at."

For example:
> "Hall's notion that 'identity is a process, not an essence' takes on a specific form in Xiaohongshu's image-text mode. Users *construct* rather than *declare* their cultural identity through a continuous stream of experience sharing. For instance, account @xxx's 12-part series of posts shows..."

This paragraph's first sentence stitches together the theory (Hall) and the data type (identity construction under Xiaohongshu's image-text mode) in one go. The reader doesn't have to cross the gulf between abstraction and concreteness.

**Researcher:**
This writing approach is great. I usually put theory at the beginning of the paragraph and data at the end, with "however" or "specifically" in between. But what you're calling an anchor sentence forces me to think clearly at the start of each paragraph: "what is the core connection of this paragraph."

**Guide:**
Exactly. One more style issue (§15). Who do you imagine your reader to be?

**Researcher:**
I hope people in cultural studies can understand it, and people in media studies can understand it too. But if I write it too much like "cultural studies" (heavy on theoretical citations, conceptual juxtapositions), people in media studies might find it too abstract. If I write it too much like "media studies" (data analysis, comparison tables), people in cultural studies might find it too shallow.

**Guide:**
This is a real dilemma. One solution is to use the introduction to establish a core question that both types of readers can understand, then write the main body of the argument on the principle that "cultural studies readers also need data, media studies readers also need theory."

In other words, don't assume "the theory section only serves cultural studies readers, the data section only serves media studies readers." Every section should provide enough theoretical anchoring and data support for both types of readers to follow along.

**Guide:**

**Researcher:**
I want to use a comparison table to show the generation × platform content coding results. The frequency of themes across four conditions (first generation/Xiaohongshu, first generation/Instagram, second generation/Xiaohongshu, second generation/Instagram). **Researcher:**

**Guide:**

Highlight the column you most want readers to notice (for example, the "second generation/Xiaohongshu" vs. "second generation/Instagram" difference). Choose screenshots that best illustrate the pattern of difference you're describing. Not necessarily the most extreme examples, but the most typical ones.

## Round 16

> 💡 **Full-process review. The researcher has gone from "feeling there's nothing new" to a clear thesis blueprint with a defined theoretical position, complete evidence chain, clear writing structure, and visual strategy. Final check on three core questions: What is the research question? Why is it worth doing? Is the logic rigorous and closed-loop?**

**Guide:**
Let's do the final summary. Looking back at the entire conversation. What is the core research question of your study?

**Researcher:**
The core question is how do social media platforms' technological affordances mediate the differences in cultural identity construction between second-generation and first-generation Chinese immigrants? More specifically: intergenerational difference is not simply "two generations being different," but different types of identity construction mechanisms formed under different platform environments.

**Guide:**
Why is this question worth doing?

**Researcher:**
Because existing literature either talks about intergenerational differences (but doesn't look at media change) or discusses digital identity (but doesn't do intergenerational comparison). Combining the two reveals a blind spot in "cross-cultural adaptation" theory. Young immigrants' identity construction is not about "choosing between two cultures," but grows naturally out of the specific technological conditions of particular platforms. If this argument holds, it would affect not only our understanding of immigrant identity but also the way we study cultural identity in the digital age. Methodologically, it shifts from "only looking at content" to "content × platform conditions."

**Guide:**
And your argument logic. What's the part you're most confident about and the part you're least confident about?

**Researcher:**
Most confident: the logic of the comparative design. The generation × platform double comparison allows me to control both "generation" and "media" variables simultaneously. Least confident: the argument mainly relies on qualitative analysis. Although the thematic coding is systematic, there's still an inferential gap between the coding and the interpretive leap that "platforms orchestrate identity." How do I determine that "the platform makes users do this," rather than "different users who were already different chose different platforms"? This is a self-selection problem.

**Guide:**
This is your core methodological challenge. Have you thought about how to address this gap in the paper?

**Researcher:**
Two lines. First, behavioral differences of the same person across two platforms (if I can find dual-platform users) can directly control for the confounding variable of "user differences." Second, if I'm mainly doing group comparison (different users on different platforms), I need to explicitly acknowledge this methodological limitation in the discussion, while using the affordance theory framework to explain that even with self-selection effects, the platform's technological conditions amplify and shape specific modes of expression. I don't need to perfectly solve this problem, but I need to let the reader know I'm aware of it.
