# Science Test Case: Craft of Research Skill Full Pipeline Test

> **Scene:** Environmental Microbiology / Soil Biogeochemistry
> **Researcher Profile:** A doctoral student studying the effects of different agricultural management practices on soil microbial communities and carbon sequestration, with preliminary data but feeling "unable to articulate the innovation"
> **Test Goal:** Run through Dimension 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 5 → 6 → Self-Check, verifying the skill's guiding effectiveness in a science research context
> **Estimated Duration:** 12-14 Rounds
> **Difficulty:** ⭐⭐⭐

---

## Round 1

> 💡 **Hard Gate. The Guide starts with open-ended questions to locate the researcher's current state, without imposing any framework. First understand "where the researcher stands," then decide which direction to guide.**

**Guide:**
Can you describe your current research status in one or two sentences? For example:

- Are you just entering this field, still reading the literature to find an entry point?
- Do you have a preliminary idea but are unsure if it has value?
- Do you already have data/experiments but don't know how to build an argument?
- Or some other state. Just say it. It doesn't need to be formal.

**Researcher:**
I have experimental data. I did metagenomic sequencing of soil microbial communities under different tillage practices (conventional tillage vs. no-till vs. reduced tillage) and found that under no-till, certain microbial taxa (such as arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi and some actinobacteria) were significantly more abundant, while soil organic carbon content was also higher. But I'm not sure whether this counts as a "discovery." It feels like two observations placed side by side without actually establishing a connection.

---

## Round 2

> 💡 **The researcher has data but is unsure of its significance → Introduce the Three-Part Frame (Dimension 1) to help transform "two observations" into "a question worth answering." The Three-Part Frame is a starting tool; it's normal if the third sentence is unclear.**

**Guide:**
"Two observations placed side by side." That phrasing itself is a clue. You have data; you just haven't yet found the story that ties them together.

Let's do a positioning exercise. Use three sentences to help yourself clarify where you stand:

> I study ______, because I want to find out ______, in order to help better understand ______.

Fill in the first two sentences. The third ("in order to…") can be intuitive; it doesn't need to be precise.

**Researcher:**
I study the effects of agricultural management practices on soil microbial communities, because I want to find out why no-till increases soil carbon: is it driven by changes in the microbial community, primarily by physical protection mechanisms, or both?

**Guide:**
Good. Now the third sentence: after understanding this, what would it help people better understand?

**Researcher:**
In order to help better understand whether soil carbon sequestration is primarily a "physical process" or a "biological process." The answer to this question would affect how we design carbon-sequestering agricultural practices.

---

## Round 3

> 💡 **The researcher's initial positioning is successful, but "two mechanisms" is still a multiple-choice question → Introduce Creative Framework Scanning to help them map their relationship to existing research and turn a multiple-choice question into "a position to take."**

**Guide:**
This positioning is good. Now use a Creative Framework to look at your relationship with existing research.

In the literature you've read, regarding "why no-till increases soil carbon," what are the mainstream explanations?

**Researcher:**
There are mainly two schools. One (represented by Six et al.) emphasizes physical protection: aggregate formation physically "hides" carbon so microbes can't degrade it. The other (represented by the microbial carbon pump concept from Liang and Liang) emphasizes that microbial necromass itself is the main contributor to stable organic carbon, so changes in microbial community composition directly determine carbon sequestration.

**Guide:**
So do you agree with both schools? Or do you think one of them doesn't hold up?

My observation is that your data happens to touch on both mechanisms simultaneously: you found changes in microbial taxa (AMF, actinobacteria) as well as changes in total carbon. Which one do you most want to challenge, or do you want to build a bridge?

**Researcher:**
I want to build a bridge. My intuition is that these two schools aren't opposed. Microbial community change and physical protection might be different stages of the same process: microbial activity generates aggregates, and aggregates in turn protect microbial necromass. But existing research rarely measures both mechanisms within the same experimental system.

---

## Round 4

> 💡 **The researcher chose the direction of "creative synthesis" (bridge-building) → Enter Dimension 2's So What? Cascade. This is the book's core tool. It helps the researcher advance from "what I found" to "why this matters."**

**Guide:**
Good, so you're not just "observing a correlation," but proposing a theoretical hypothesis that connects two mechanisms.

Now let's run the So What? Cascade. Don't worry. It's not an exam. It's a step-by-step process to help you see why your research matters.

**Layer 1:** "I found that under no-till systems, the abundance of certain microbial taxa is positively correlated with soil carbon content, and I propose that this might be because microbial activity drives aggregate formation, and aggregates protect microbial necromass carbon."

→ **So what?**

If a colleague heard you say this, why should they care?

**Researcher:**
Well… because if this mechanism is real, it means no-till carbon sequestration is not a passive physical process but an actively regulable biological process. You could optimize the microbial community through agricultural management to maximize carbon sequestration.

**Guide:**
Second layer emerged. Continue.

→ **So "can be actively regulated." Then what?**

**Researcher:**
Then: our current carbon models (like RothC, CENTURY) basically treat soil carbon as a passive pool simulated primarily with physicochemical parameters. If microbial community structure determines the upper limit of carbon sequestration, these models are missing a key biological variable.

**Guide:**
Third layer. And further?

→ **Missing a variable. Then what?**

**Researcher:**
That means we might be underestimating the carbon sequestration potential of some management practices while overestimating others. This has implications for carbon sink accounting, carbon credits, and agricultural climate policy.

> 💡 **So What Cascade reaches the fifth layer (policy/practice layer) → naturally stops. The researcher can continue to the final layer on their own. The chain of significance is clear. Key test passed: the research neither elicits "I don't care" nor leads to an unanswerable "so what?" Instead, it cascades layer by layer to the policy and practice level.**

---

## Round 5

> 💡 **After the significance chain is established → Use the "I Don't Care" check (§1.3) as a stress test, while introducing the C-P-R Framework (§14) to help the researcher structure their expression. C-P-R is the key bridge between "thinking clearly" and "explaining clearly."**

**Guide:**
Good. Now let's do a check.

If you presented your core question at an academic conference: "Are microbial community change and physical protection actually two stages of the same process?" Would the audience respond with "I don't care"?

**Researcher:**
I think… people working on soil carbon would care, and people working on microbiology would care too. But agricultural management researchers might find it too fundamental. They'd ask "So how does this actually guide practice?"

**Guide:**
That's a good observation. You've actually identified two types of readers already: basic scientists and applied scientists. The key is how to speak to both audiences simultaneously in your paper.

Now use the C-P-R Framework to condense your research into a 30-second elevator pitch:

- **Context:** "Regarding agricultural soil carbon sequestration, we now know that both microbes and aggregates matter, but…"
- **Problem:** "But we don't understand the relationship between these two mechanisms. Are they independent, competing, or different stages of the same process?"
- **Response:** "So in the same experimental system, I simultaneously measured microbial community structure and aggregate distribution, to see how they influence each other."

Try filling it out. It doesn't need to be perfect. Just say what comes intuitively.

**Researcher:**
Context: we know no-till increases organic carbon and changes the microbial community. Problem: the "physical protection" and "microbial-driven" mechanisms under no-till have always been studied separately, with no one measuring them simultaneously. Response: I measured both sets of indicators at the same time, to see whether microbial community changes can predict aggregate distribution and carbon content changes.

---

## Round 6

> 💡 **The researcher can clearly articulate their research story → Enter Dimension 3 (Argument Building), starting with claim refinement. Key guidance: help the researcher distinguish between "what I observed" and "what I want to convince readers to believe": this is the leap from data to argument.**

**Guide:**
Good. Now what is your claim? Not your experimental question. What do you want to convince readers to believe using your evidence?

Use the "Although/Even though" pattern to write your core claim. This simultaneously clarifies what you are challenging:

> Although…, …is the case, because…

**Researcher:**
Although physical protection mechanisms are considered abiotic-driven, microbial community structure may be the initial driver of aggregate formation and carbon sequestration, because the changes in microbial community under no-till are statistically highly correlated with changes in aggregate distribution.

**Guide:**
That claim is not weak. Let's unpack it.

You've said three things that need evidence:
1. The microbial community did change under no-till
2. Aggregate distribution and carbon content did change under no-till
3. There is a causal relationship between microbial change and aggregate change, not just a correlation

You have evidence for the first two: your metagenomic and carbon data. The third is your core argument and also your weak spot. Correlation alone cannot prove causation.

You used the word "because," but correlation is not causation. What type of evidence could help you support causation?

> 💡 **The researcher's "because" reveals an implicit inferential leap from correlation to causation. The Guide does not criticize this leap but lets the researcher realize the mismatch in evidence type on their own.**

**Researcher:**
Yeah, currently I can only speak to correlation, not directly to causation. To establish causality, I'd probably need a microcosm experiment: inoculating different microbial communities into sterile soil to see whether aggregate formation differs.

**Guide:**
That becomes a two-stage argument. Within your paper's framework, do you want to use your current data to tell a story of "strong correlation + mechanistic hypothesis," or wait until the microcosm experiment is done before submitting?

This is a question of paper positioning. If your current data only shows correlation, then in your "Although/Even though" claim, the third part needs to change from "because…" to "this suggests…" or "this provides evidence for the hypothesis that…"

**Researcher:**
I understand. Let me revise:

Although physical protection mechanisms are considered abiotic-driven, our data suggests that microbial community structure may be a precursor factor for aggregate formation, because under no-till, the magnitude of microbial community change is highly correlated with changes in aggregate mean weight diameter (MWD), and AMF abundance can predict the proportion of >250μm macroaggregates.

---

## Round 7

> 💡 **Claim refined (from "because" to "suggests") → Enter the Warrant test. The Guide uses the If-Then Test to expose the researcher's unstated causal assumption.**

**Guide:**
This version uses more precise qualifiers. "Suggests," "highly correlated," and "predict" match the evidence you have.

But you're still assuming a key premise: that the correlation reflects a general mechanism. Let's expose it.

**If-Then Test:**

If you say "AMF abundance predicts macroaggregate proportion," then: if I measured the same parameters in another set of samples (different soil type, different climate zone), would your correlation still hold?

What assumption are you making?

**Researcher:**
I'm assuming that AMF promotion of aggregate formation is a general mechanism, not just valid at my experimental site.

**Guide:**
Exactly. That's your warrant. You need to defend it by finding mechanistic evidence already established in other systems from the literature (AMF hyphal entanglement and glomalin protein effects on aggregates), while acknowledging that your contribution lies in the "simultaneous measurement of both sets of indicators within the same experimental system" design.

Now let's do a complete argument planning exercise:

Before readers can accept that "microbial community structure is a driver of no-till carbon sequestration, not a byproduct," ask: what three things do they need to believe first?

---

## Round 8

> 💡 **Argument planning → Leads to Objection Anticipation (§9). A good researcher must not only articulate their own argument but also anticipate how opponents will attack it.**

**Researcher:**
Three things:
1. No-till changed the microbial community structure
2. No-till also changed aggregate distribution and carbon content
3. The correlation between these two is not coincidental, not caused by a third factor

I have evidence for the first two. For the third, I currently only have statistical correlation and need to control for confounding variables.

**Guide:**
Good. The third is the weakest point. Think ahead: what could be challenged?

**Researcher:**
The most direct challenge is: "The correlation you observed might be caused by a third factor, such as differences in residue input. No-till leaves more crop residue on the surface, which both provides substrate for microbes (changing the community) and directly contributes organic carbon upon decomposition. So the microbial change and carbon increase could be two parallel outcomes rather than a causal relationship."

**Guide:**
That's a high-quality challenge. How do you plan to respond?

**Researcher:**
Two directions. First, if it were simply a matter of differential residue input, I should see all microbial taxa increasing, but in fact only specific taxa (AMF, actinobacteria, some Gram-negative bacteria) increased, while some taxa (such as certain Proteobacteria) actually decreased. This suggests it's not simply "more substrate → more microbes." Second, I can include residue input as a covariate in my statistical analysis to see whether, after controlling for the residue effect, the partial correlation between microbial variables and aggregate variables remains significant.

---

## Round 9

> 💡 **Argument building is essentially complete → Enter Ethics (Dimension 4) and Evolution (Dimension 5). These two dimensions do not need lengthy treatment but test whether the researcher has "research big-picture thinking."**

**Guide:**
The argument framework is solid. Let's quickly go through two dimensions.

**Ethics (Dimension 4):** What ethical considerations does your research have?

**Researcher:**
Primarily data integrity. Metagenomic data processing involves many parameter choices (different quality control thresholds, different database annotations), and I need to explain the basis for these choices in the methods section. Additionally, if I advocate for no-till carbon sequestration, I need to simultaneously clarify the conditions under which no-till might reduce yield or increase pesticide use, not selectively present only favorable results.

**Guide:**
Good. **Next step (Dimension 5):** If your core hypothesis is accepted, that microbial structural change is the initial driver of no-till carbon sequestration, what are the most interesting new questions that arise?

**Researcher:**
That opens up several directions: First, can this effect be reproduced through microbial inoculation (e.g., commercial AMF inoculants) under different management practices? Second, where are the thresholds across different climate zones and soil types: under what conditions does microbial driving dominate, and under what conditions does physical protection dominate? Third, can this mechanism be parameterized and incorporated into carbon models?

**Guide:**
These three directions alone could generate sub-projects. You've gone from a "correlation observation" to a framework that can grow into a systematic research program.

## Round 10

> 💡 **Core argument + significance + ethics + evolution all successfully navigated → Use the Innovation Self-Check for a full-process review (Step 1-4). The goal is not perfection in every step. The key is that the researcher knows where they are weak and how to address it.**

**Guide:**
Finally, let's go through the Innovation Self-Check.

**Step 1. Positioning: Who am I in conversation with?**
- Can you articulate the core question being debated in this field?
- Do you know the positions of 2-3 key researchers?
- Is your research a response to some existing claim?

**Researcher:**
Yes. Core question: What is the microbial mechanism of soil carbon sequestration? Key positions: physical protection school vs. microbial carbon pump school. My research responds to "Are these two independent or two aspects of the same process?" I'm building a bridge.

**Guide:**
**Step 2. Significance: Why should anyone care?**
- Can you naturally answer "So What?" for at least 2 layers?
- Will your research change your audience's understanding?

**Researcher:**
Yes. It goes beyond "adding another case study of no-till carbon sequestration" to challenge the default assumption that "physical protection is an abiotic process," proposing that microbes are the driving force. If this is right, our models and policies would need adjustment.

**Guide:**
**Step 3. Argument Tension Test: Weakest point?**
- Can you distinguish between reasons and evidence?
- Do you know the most likely point of attack?

**Researcher:**
The weakest is causal direction. I currently only have correlation, so I used qualifiers like "suggests" and "predicts," while anticipating possible confounding variables (residue input) and a statistical control plan in advance. But causal proof does require the next experiment.

**Guide:**
**Step 4. Audience Calibration: Who would oppose?**
- Can you restate your core argument from an opponent's perspective?

**Researcher:**
The opponent's position: "Both the microbial community change and the carbon increase are byproducts of no-till; there is no causal relationship between them." My response: The byproduct hypothesis cannot explain why it is specifically certain microbial taxa that change, nor why AMF abundance can predict macroaggregate proportion. But this indeed requires a microcosm experiment for final confirmation.

**Guide:**
All four steps successfully completed. Step 5 (Research Consequences) you've already naturally answered earlier.

## Round 11

> 💡 **Self-Check completed → Enter Dimension 6 (Delivery): writing planning. The researcher already has a clear argument structure, but "putting it into a paper" is another matter. The Guide focuses on organizational structure (§11) and narrative arc (§12), testing whether the researcher can translate their thinking process into a path readers can follow. This is also the ultimate test of "logical closure." If you can't write it clearly, you haven't thought it through.**

**Guide:**
Good. Now the content of your argument is essentially formed. Let's look at how to write it up.

Your core question is to convince readers that "microbial community structure is not a byproduct of no-till carbon sequestration, but a driving factor." So how should this paper be structured?

In other words, what do readers need to know first, before they can understand your core claim?

**Researcher:**
I think readers first need to understand the background: why the mechanism of no-till carbon sequestration matters, and what the current "physical protection" vs. "microbial-driven" debate is about. Then my experimental design: why I measured both microbial and aggregate indicators simultaneously. Then the results: first showing that no-till changes the microbial community, then showing that no-till changes aggregate distribution, and finally linking the two with a correlation analysis. Then the discussion and conclusion.

**Guide:**
This structure is essentially the standard IMRaD format of "Background → Methods → Results → Discussion." But there's a problem with your story: your core claim is about "bridge-building," connecting two mechanisms, yet your structure presents them separately (first microbial results, then aggregate results), only connecting them in the discussion.

Have you considered adjusting it: interweaving them in the results section? For example, present in the order of "Treatment → Microbial response → Aggregate response → Correlation," so that at each step the reader sees the correspondence between the two data sets?

> 💡 **The Guide is testing for a common writing trap: the researcher's thinking is linear (first A, then B → conclusion C), but readers need progressive logic (each mini-conclusion pushes the argument one step forward). The IMRaD structure is not always the best way to tell the story.**

**Researcher:**
You're right. If I follow standard IMRaD, I'd essentially place the most critical "connection" in the final section, and readers might be exhausted by the time they get there. I could adjust to organize the results section directly around a "hypothesis-driven structure": Part 1 showing "what no-till changed (microbial + aggregate changes individually)," and Part 2 showing "the relationship between these two changes (correlation analysis)." This way, readers would already see in the results section that "the changes didn't happen independently. They're correlated."

**Guide:**
That adjustment is good. Now let's talk about the narrative arc (§12). Good academic argument needs not only logic but also a narrative arc. Going back to the beginning. What困惑 (puzzle) did your research start from?

**Researcher:**
The puzzle came when I was doing my experiments: I noticed in the literature that the "physical protection school" and the "microbial carbon pump school" were each talking to their own data sets and rarely looking at what the other side was doing. But my data showed signs of both mechanisms simultaneously. This contradiction made me think they might actually be the same thing.

**Guide:**
This puzzle itself should be written into the introduction. The opening of your paper is not "Soil carbon sequestration is important" but "We have two explanations, but they shouldn't be separate." This puzzle is your narrative starting point; it grabs readers more effectively than a statement of "importance."

## Round 12

> 💡 **Entering the concrete level of writing: visual strategy (§16). In science papers, figures are often what reviewers look at first. The Guide tests whether the researcher knows whether each figure is "evidentiary" or "rhetorical."**

**Guide:**
Now consider a concrete question: what figures will your paper contain? What is the core message of each figure?

**Researcher:**
I'm thinking there would be several figures: Figure 1 is an experimental design and treatment schematic, Figure 2 shows microbial community composition (PCoA + key taxa abundance bar chart), Figure 3 shows aggregate distribution and carbon content, and Figure 4 is a correlation heatmap.

**Guide:**
Among these figures: if you removed Figure 1, could readers still understand your core argument?

**Researcher:**
Yes… Figure 1 just shows the experimental design; it's not itself part of the argument.

**Guide:**
Then Figure 1 is rhetorical, not evidentiary. It aids understanding but doesn't carry the argument. What about Figure 4?

**Researcher:**
Figure 4 is central. Without Figure 4, readers can't see the correlation between microbes and aggregates. It's evidentiary.

**Guide:**
Right. Now you can make the distinction. What core message do you want Figure 4 to convey?

**Researcher:**
The positive correlation between AMF abundance and >250μm macroaggregate proportion is the strongest signal. That's the relationship I most want people to remember.

**Guide:**
That figure should be designed around this "strongest signal." Use visual elements to highlight it, don't make readers search for it among a pile of correlations. Color, annotation, orientation: all visual elements should serve to make this relationship graspable within one second.

Also, one more question about writing style (§15). What are the most likely problems in your first draft?

**Researcher:**
I'd guess two problems: overly heavy passive voice ("Soil samples were collected… DNA was extracted…") and abstract subjects ("The results showed…"). Needs revision.

**Guide:**
Use concrete agents as subjects ("We collected soil samples"), and replace "results" with your analytical actions ("Correlation analysis revealed"). This not only improves clarity but also represents a form of academic integrity. You explicitly state who did what.

> 💡 **Dimension 6 test complete. The researcher not only knows "what to write" but also "how to organize" and "how figures serve the argument." Writing is not an afterthought. How to present it should be considered from the argument stage onward.**

---

## Round 13

> 💡 **Full-process review. The researcher has gone from "I have data but don't know if it counts as a discovery" to a complete paper framework. All six dimensions + self-check engine navigated. Final check on three core questions: What is the scientific question? Why is it worth doing? Is the reasoning rigorous and closed-loop?**

**Guide:**
Let's do a final summary. Looking back at the entire dialogue: what is the core of your research?

**Researcher:**
The core scientific question is: under no-till conditions, is microbial community structure change a driver or a byproduct of soil carbon increase? My answer: our data suggests it is a driver because microbial change is highly correlated with aggregate change, specific microbial taxa (AMF) can predict aggregate distribution, and this correlation cannot be fully explained by the confounding variable of "residue input."

**Guide:**
Why is this research worth doing?

**Researcher:**
Because if it's correct, it unifies "physical protection" and "microbial driving" (two mechanisms long studied separately) into different stages of the same process, thereby changing our understanding of soil carbon sequestration mechanisms: from a passive physicochemical process to an actively regulable biological process. This would directly affect the parameter systems of carbon models, the design of carbon-sequestering agricultural practices, and carbon sink accounting methodologies.

**Guide:**
The logical closure of your argument: what part gives you the most confidence and what part gives you the least?

**Researcher:**
Most confident: the logic of the experimental design, simultaneously measuring both sets of indicators within the same experimental system. Least confident: the determination of causal direction. My data is persuasive in the strength of correlation, but I cannot methodologically claim that "microbial change precedes aggregate change" because I don't have a time-series experiment. I've addressed this gap with qualifiers ("suggests," "predicts") and specified the need for microcosm experiments in the discussion and future directions.

**Guide:**
This is a rigorous closure. It shows what the argument achieved, what it didn't, and accounts for what hasn't been achieved.

> 💡 **All three core questions have clear answers. The researcher has not only articulated "what to do" and "why it matters" but also accurately identified the weak points in the logical closure and their handling strategies. Science test case: full pipeline passed.**
